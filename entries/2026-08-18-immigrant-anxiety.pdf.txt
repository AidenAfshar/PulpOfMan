Anxiety in second generation immigrants

Immigrant parents come to America hoping to give their children a chance at a good life. They rebuild their life in this new country, and work hard to do so. They’ve seen how those around them who didn’t work as hard stumble into oblivion. So they tell their children they must strive high to live the good life.
Only, the children have not seen what their parents have. They did not have to rebuild their lives from scratch, so they have the freedom to lollygag, socialize, and drift along the current of the culture they live in. The pressure their parents felt, and needed, to succeed doesn’t quite translate to the tranquility of suburban life. So perhaps they need not fear the world like their parents did
But those parents, having witnessed family and friends ruin their lives over not having worked hard enough in their youths, will likely see a child's lack of urgency as the beginning of their own ruin. Thus, they go to great lengths to impress the gravity of the matter - the absolute need to do what the parents say is right.
Thus, a strict pattern develops, where the child must anxiously try to avoid falling into the traps their parents warned them of. They teeter back and forth into safety and taboo, trying to please their parents without losing out on the pleasures of the world they live in. They become engulfed in an effort to fit within the incongruent groundwork their parents while adhering to what life demands and offers - an endless, anxious, uphill battle.
If this is you, I want you to consider “amor fati,” the love of one’s fate. I want you to choose to believe that life is worth living, no matter what it looks like. And you may find that life is wonderful even in the depths of what your parents taught you is absolute "wrong." So go forth, go out of bounds, and discover all life has to offer.
